iOS development is making apps for the iPhone and iPad with Apple's tools: a Mac, Xcode, the Swift language, and a
paid developer account once you want your app on anyone else's phone. Cole ships native Swift apps for the iPhone,
the Apple Watch and the Mac, and most of that Swift is written by agents running in [[claude-code|Claude Code]] and
[[codex|Codex]]. This page is the map: what you need, what it costs, how a build travels from a laptop to a phone,
and where the sharp edges are. Each stop on the way has its own page.

## The whole path in seven steps

1. **Get a Mac and install [[xcode|Xcode]].** Nothing else builds, signs and uploads an iPhone app.
2. **Pick an approach.** Native Swift, a web app wrapped with Capacitor, or React Native with Expo. The trade-offs are
   in [[capacitor-expo-swift|Capacitor vs Expo vs Swift]].
3. **Run it on your own phone, for free.** A free Apple Account is enough to install your app on your own iPhone from
   Xcode, with weekly limits: see [[push-to-your-phone|pushing straight to your phone]].
4. **Join the Apple Developer Program** (below) when you want other people to run it.
5. **Create the app in [[app-store-connect|App Store Connect]]**, Apple's console for builds, testers, store pages and
   review.
6. **Upload builds and beta test with [[testflight|TestFlight]]**: a private group of your own team, then a public
   link once the build passes Beta App Review.
7. **Submit for App Review and release**, all at once or in phases.

The same machinery, with small differences, ships the other things in this part of the wiki:
[[mac-apps|Mac and menu bar apps]], [[apple-watch-apps|Apple Watch apps]] and [[safari-extensions|Safari extensions]].

## What you need

### A Mac

Xcode runs only on macOS, so every iPhone app is built, signed and uploaded on a Mac, even when the code was written
somewhere else. Xcode 27, the current major version, needs macOS Tahoe 26.6 or later. In Cole's setup, agents write
code on the [[servers|AWS servers]] and the laptop, and the builds that need Apple's toolchain run on the [[mac-mini|Mac mini]]
on the desk. A new base Mac mini is <Price id="mac-mini" />.

There are two ways around owning the Mac that does the build. GitHub's hosted macOS runners can build and sign in
[[github-actions|GitHub Actions]] (free for public repositories, billed per minute for private ones at
<Price id="github-actions-macos-minute" />, about ten times the Linux rate). And Expo's EAS Build compiles React
Native apps on Expo's own Macs. You still want a Mac nearby for the simulator and for debugging.

### Xcode

[[xcode|Xcode]] is free. It bundles the Swift compiler, the iOS SDK, the simulators, Instruments, the code signing
machinery and the upload tools. As of late October 2026 the current release is Xcode 27.1 (Swift 6.4, the iOS 27.1
SDK), with 27.2 at release candidate. It debugs on devices running iOS 17 or later.

### An Apple Account, and later the program

A free Apple Account, signed in to Xcode, gives you a *Personal Team*: enough to run your own app on your own
phone. Anything that reaches other people (TestFlight, the App Store, signed Mac apps) needs the paid
[Apple Developer Program](https://developer.apple.com/programs/).

### An iPhone

The simulator is fast and good for most UI work, but Apple says it plainly: simulators "don't replicate the
performance or features of a physical device." Cameras, sensors, hardware buttons, push notifications and real
networking all need a real phone. One of Cole's apps added a camera feature driven by the volume buttons, and the
only way to test it was a TestFlight build on a physical iPhone, because the simulator cannot press them.

## The Apple Developer Program

The developer fee is <Price id="apple-developer-program" />, renewed every membership year, and Apple notes the price
may vary by region. Here is what it changes, from Apple's own comparison and capability tables:

| | Free Apple Account (Personal Team) | Apple Developer Program |
|---|---|---|
| Xcode, simulators, beta OS releases | Yes | Yes |
| Run your app on your own devices | Yes: up to 3 devices and 3 apps per device, and the app IDs, devices and profiles all expire after 7 days | Yes, without the weekly expiry; up to 100 registered devices per product family per membership year |
| [[testflight\|TestFlight]] betas | No | Up to 100 internal and 10,000 external testers |
| The App Store | No | Yes, through [[app-store-connect\|App Store Connect]] |
| Push notifications, iCloud, Sign in with Apple, Associated Domains, Apple Pay | No | Yes |
| App Groups, Keychain Sharing, HealthKit, background modes | Yes | Yes |
| [[mac-apps\|Mac apps]] signed with Developer ID and notarized | No | Yes |
| Xcode Cloud | No | 25 compute hours a month included |

Two things to decide before you enroll:

- **Individual or organization.** An individual enrolls with their legal name, and that name appears as the seller
  on the App Store. An organization must be a legal entity with a D-U-N-S number and a working website, and its name
  appears instead. Apple does not accept trade names or DBAs, so a sole developer who wants a company name on the
  store needs an actual company first.
- **The Apple Account you use.** It needs two-factor authentication and your real first and last name; Apple says an
  alias or a company name in those fields delays approval.

Nonprofits, accredited schools and government entities can ask for a fee waiver. Organizations that distribute apps
only to their own employees have a separate Enterprise Program, which comes without App Store Connect or TestFlight,
so it cannot publish to the App Store.

Pay for the developer program the day you want somebody else to try your app. Until then, a free account and a
cable get your app onto your own phone, and that covers a lot of building.

## Choosing how to build

There are three serious options, compared in depth in [[capacitor-expo-swift|Capacitor vs Expo vs Swift]]:

- **Native Swift**, with SwiftUI and UIKit. Every Apple API on the day it ships, native performance, and the only
  first-class route to widgets, Apple Watch apps and Safari extensions.
- **Capacitor**, which wraps an existing web app in a native shell. Fastest if the website already exists, with the
  risk that App Review sees "a repackaged website."
- **Expo**, the framework React Native itself recommends: one TypeScript codebase for iOS and Android, rendered with
  real native views, built and submitted from Expo's cloud.

Cole's public Apple apps are all native Swift. [Drover's native folder](https://github.com/coletebou/drover/tree/master/native)
holds an iPhone app, an Apple Watch app, a Mac app, a menu bar app, home-screen widgets and a Safari extension, all
generated from one [XcodeGen](https://github.com/yonaskolb/XcodeGen) spec and sharing one Swift package, DroverKit.
[Tide](https://github.com/coletebou/tide) is a code editor for iPad and iPhone in the spirit of VS Code, written in
Swift with no web view at all. One of Cole's apps began as a Capacitor wrapper around its website, and even that one
is native Swift on the phone today; [[capacitor-expo-swift#what-cole-runs|what Cole runs]] has the reasoning.

## How a build reaches a phone

There are four ways to get an app onto an iPhone, from most hands-on to most public:

| Route | Who can install | Needs | Page |
|---|---|---|---|
| Run from Xcode | Your own devices, connected by cable or Wi-Fi | Developer Mode on the device; a free or paid account | [[push-to-your-phone]] |
| Ad hoc | Devices whose identifiers you registered | The paid program | [[push-to-your-phone#ad-hoc-builds\|Ad hoc builds]] |
| TestFlight | Your team (internal) or anyone you invite or who has the public link (external) | The paid program; Beta App Review for external testers | [[testflight]] |
| The App Store | Everyone | App Review | [[app-store-connect]] |

Every upload to App Store Connect carries two numbers: the version people see (1.4) and a build number. A build
number can be used only once, so a team with several people or several agents cutting builds needs one place that
hands them out. Once, another of Cole's sessions had already uploaded the build number a release was about to use;
since then the release script asks App Store Connect whether a build number is taken before it uploads.

## Two release clocks

The biggest change when an app gets a native client is that the code now ships on two clocks. A server fix is live
the moment it merges and deploys. A fix in the iPhone app is live only after a build is archived, uploaded, processed,
put in front of testers, reviewed, released, and then installed by each user, some of whom never update. In Cole's
setup this shows up as a queue: server fixes ship on merge, while iPhone fixes collect as numbered follow-ups until
the next TestFlight build.

A few habits make the slower clock easier to live with:

- **Keep the server compatible with old app builds.** Old versions stay in use for months. Add fields; don't rename
  or remove them until the old builds are gone.
- **Put switches on the server.** A feature the server can turn on or off, per user or for everyone, does not need a
  new build to launch or to roll back.
- **Cut a crash fix from the build that crashed.** If a TestFlight build crashes and `master` has moved on with
  unproven work, base the fix on the shipped build's commit plus the fix, then merge it back, so the emergency build
  carries nothing untested.
- **Write release notes that claim only what you verified.** TestFlight's "What to Test" text is what testers rely on.

## How agents fit in

Cole's iPhone code is mostly written by agents, so the project is shaped for them:

- **The project file is generated.** Both [[Drover]] and Tide keep a `project.yml` for XcodeGen as the source of truth and
  generate the Xcode project from it. Agents edit a short YAML file instead of a machine-written `.pbxproj`, and two
  branches that add files no longer conflict in it. The catch: a new Swift file only joins the build after
  `xcodegen generate` runs, so "the tests pass but Xcode can't find the type" means the project was not regenerated.
- **The logic lives in a plain Swift package.** Tide's `TideCore` and Drover's `DroverKit` have no UI dependencies,
  so `swift test` runs in seconds on a Mac, and part of DroverKit's suite even runs on Linux in CI.
- **Agents can drive Xcode itself.** Since Xcode 26.3, Xcode exposes its build, test and preview tools to outside
  agents through [[mcp|MCP]]. One command connects Claude Code to the open project:

  ```sh
  claude mcp add --transport stdio xcode -- xcrun mcpbridge
  ```

  Xcode 27 lets agents boot simulators, install and launch apps, tap the screen and take screenshots, and it accepts
  plugins that bundle [[skills-and-plugins|skills]] and MCP servers. Details are on the [[xcode]] page.
- **Some steps stay human.** Accepting the Xcode license, unlocking the keychain that holds a signing identity, signing
  in to App Store Connect in a browser, and pressing real buttons on a real phone all wait for Cole.

## Builds in CI

[[github-actions|GitHub Actions]] can build and test iPhone apps on hosted macOS runners. Two of Cole's public
repositories show the range:

- **Tide** runs its core tests, an iOS simulator build, and a full UI test suite on an iPad simulator on every push,
  and keeps the UI tests' screenshots as an artifact.
- **Drover** builds every scheme unsigned on pushes and pull requests, and has a separate, manual `signed` workflow
  that signs with a certificate imported into a throwaway keychain, notarizes the Mac apps, and uploads the iPhone
  app to TestFlight using an App Store Connect API key. That workflow never lets Xcode create certificates on its own, because a fresh
  CI runner otherwise mints a new development certificate on every run until the account hits Apple's limit.

Hosted macOS minutes are free on public repositories and expensive on private ones, so Cole keeps private macOS
jobs lean.

## Current requirements

As of late October 2026:

- Apps uploaded to App Store Connect must be built with Xcode 26 or later against an iOS 26 (or matching platform)
  SDK, a rule in force since April 28, 2026.
- iPhone and iPad apps must target iOS 13 or later (since September 9, 2026).
- Xcode 27 and 27.1 need macOS Tahoe 26.6 or later on the Mac that builds.
- The App Store's age ratings are 4+, 9+, 13+, 16+ and 18+, and every app had to answer Apple's updated age-rating
  questions by January 31, 2026.

Apple posts these on its [upcoming requirements](https://developer.apple.com/news/upcoming-requirements/) page; check
it before a release, because the minimum SDK moves every spring.

## Gotchas

- **Beta Xcode builds don't ship.** App Store Connect refused an archive Cole's CI built with a beta Xcode
  ("Unsupported SDK or Xcode version"); the same code built with the released Xcode uploaded fine. Build release
  candidates with a released Xcode.
- **Xcode might not be the Xcode you think.** If `xcode-select -p` points at the Command Line Tools, `xcodebuild`
  fails with "requires Xcode." Point `DEVELOPER_DIR` at the full Xcode, and note that `sudo` drops that variable,
  which matters for `sudo xcodebuild -license accept`.
- **Archives and caches are huge.** Derived data, archives and simulator runtimes eat tens of gigabytes. Cole keeps
  release work on an [[mac-mini#storage-on-external-drives|external volume]] because the Mac mini's internal disk
  fills up.
- **Simulators are memory hungry.** A booted simulator plus a large build can push a 16 GB Mac into heavy swapping
  (see [[mac-mini#living-with-16-gb|living with 16 GB]]). Cole's release scripts wait for a free slot before they
  start an archive.
- **A wrapped website can fail review.** Guideline 4.2 asks for "features, content, and UI that elevate it beyond a
  repackaged website."
- **Capabilities belong to bundle IDs.** Every widget and extension has its own bundle ID. Automatic signing in Xcode
  enables capabilities for you, but with manual signing in CI each bundle ID needs its capabilities (App Groups,
  push, time-sensitive notifications) turned on in the developer portal, or the archive fails late with a
  provisioning profile error. Drover's menu bar app hit exactly that with time-sensitive notifications.
