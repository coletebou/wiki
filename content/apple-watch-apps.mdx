An Apple Watch app is a watchOS app: SwiftUI on a screen the size of a postage stamp, with a strict budget for
running in the background. It can stand alone or ship alongside an iPhone app, and its most visible part is often not
the app at all but a complication on the watch face or a notification that taps your wrist. Cole's example is the
Drover watch app, which lists the agent sessions waiting on Cole, shows each one's last message, and answers with a
canned reply or dictation. Watch apps are built with [[xcode|Xcode]] like the rest of [[ios-development]].

## Three kinds of watch app

| Kind | What it means |
|---|---|
| Watch-only | No iPhone app. Xcode still creates a small iOS stub target, used for the bundle ID and App Store packaging, but nothing installs on the phone |
| Independent, with a companion | Ships with an iPhone app but installs and runs without it ("Supports Running Without iOS App Installation") |
| Dependent | Installs on the watch, but cannot launch until the iPhone app is installed |

Apple's advice is to make watch apps independent, because people expect them to "just work, even when they don't have
their iPhones with them," and they can buy watch apps directly from the App Store on the watch. A watch app with a
companion shares universal in-app purchases with it.

## Getting data onto the watch

A watch reaches the internet three ways, and a request can take any of them: through the paired iPhone over
Bluetooth, over a known Wi-Fi network, or over its own cellular connection. Apple asks you to test all three.

- **Use URLSession directly.** In the foreground a default session is fastest. In the background use a background
  session, which guarantees a response eventually, even if the app is suspended.
- **Treat the iPhone as a bonus.** WatchConnectivity can push data from the companion app, but it "isn't always
  available," so Apple says to use it "as an opportunistic optimization, rather than the primary means of supplying
  fresh data."
- **Expect very little background time.** Background refresh tasks are "strictly limited — both in the number of
  times your app can wake per day, and in the amount of time it can run when it wakes," and the system favors apps
  with a complication on the active watch face and apps in the dock.

## Complications are widgets

Complications, the small readouts on the watch face, are built with WidgetKit on watchOS 9 and later. ClockKit-based
complications are deprecated as of watchOS 10. Because they are WidgetKit, the same SwiftUI views can often power both
a watch complication and a Lock Screen widget on the iPhone.

## The worked example: Drover for Apple Watch

[Drover's watch app](https://github.com/coletebou/drover/blob/master/native/Apps/watchOS/README.md) is a companion
to the Drover iPhone app (SwiftUI, watchOS 10 and later, Swift 6). It shows the [[drover|Drover]] sessions waiting on
Cole and the last message of each, and replies with one tap (yes, no, continue, go ahead, status?) or with dictated
text that it asks to confirm before sending. New waiting items buzz the watch.

How it stays current, within the watch's rules:

- **In the foreground**, every time the app becomes active, it fetches the daemon's summary, writes it into the watch's
  shared container, reloads the complications, and buzzes for waiting items it has not seen before.
- **In the background**, an app-refresh task asks to run about every 15 minutes, backing off to two hours after
  errors, while watchOS decides the real schedule. A suspended watch app can only use the network through a background
  URL session, so the task queues a download; when it finishes, the system wakes the app, which writes the summary,
  reloads the complications and posts a local notification. That notification is what makes the wrist buzz when the
  app is not on screen.
- **There is no push.** The Drover daemon has no Apple push service and no push certificate, by design, so nothing
  leaves Cole's own network; the watch learns about a new item only when it refreshes.

Credentials get the same care as on the phone:

- **The iPhone hands them over.** The iPhone app sends the server address and two tokens with
  `WCSession.transferUserInfo`, which the system encrypts between the paired devices.
- **The watch has its own Keychain and its own container.** The iPhone's Keychain and App Group are not shared with
  the watch, so the tokens go into the watch's own Keychain, readable only on that device after first unlock, and the
  watch app and its complications share the watch's own App Group.
- **Least privilege by default.** Background refreshes and complications use a read-only widget token; the full token
  is used only for replies. Tokens are never logged or put in a URL, and "Unpair this watch" deletes them along with
  the cache.

The networking rule that bites: App Transport Security blocks plain HTTP to anything but local addresses, so the
watch reaches the daemon over HTTPS, which Drover's setup provides by publishing the daemon on the
[[tailscale|tailnet]] with `tailscale serve`.

For testing, the watch app's logic compiles and runs as plain tests on a Mac, a separate script compiles the sources
against the watchOS SDK to catch errors a typecheck misses, and a `--mock` launch argument runs the app on built-in
sample data for screenshots.

## Running it on your watch

- **Developer Mode on both devices.** To pair an Apple Watch with Xcode, turn on Developer Mode on the watch and on its
  paired iPhone (see [[push-to-your-phone]]).
- **Pair the iPhone first.** In Device Hub, the watch comes through its companion iPhone. Apple Watch Series 5 and
  older also need the Mac on the same Bonjour-compatible Wi-Fi network as the watch.
- **Wireless is better now.** Xcode 27 pairs iPhones and watches running iOS 27 and watchOS 27 over the network,
  which Apple says "provides a more reliable experience for pairing watches." With older iOS versions, the iPhone has
  to stay connected to the Mac by cable to run an app on any Apple Watch.
- **TestFlight works on the watch.** A watch app embedded in an iPhone app travels with it through
  [[testflight|TestFlight]], the way Drover's does.
- **Agents can test it too.** In Xcode 27, coding agents can verify watchOS apps in the simulator, "including rotating
  and pressing the Digital Crown, and pressing the side and Action buttons."

## Gotchas

- **The background budget is real.** Design for a refresh every quarter hour at best. Show the time of the last update
  instead of pretending the data is live.
- **Nothing is shared by default.** A setting saved on the iPhone does not exist on the watch until you send it, and
  that includes Keychain items.
- **Test the network routes.** A request that works through the phone can fail on Wi-Fi alone or on cellular.
- **Dictation needs a confirm step.** Speech recognition on a wrist makes mistakes; Drover shows dictated text and asks
  before sending it to a session.
- **Complications get priority.** An app whose complication is on the active face gets background time first, which
  is one more reason to ship one.
